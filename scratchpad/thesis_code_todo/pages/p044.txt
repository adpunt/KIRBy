2.5.2    Learning with noisy labels
Learning with noisy labels (LNL) has three groups of methods, set out in Table 2.3 and
Figure 2.3. Song et al. [51] sort deep learning methods for noisy labels into five groups:
robust architectures, robust regularization, robust loss functions, loss adjustment and
sample selection.

Table 2.3: Terms used throughout this thesis for methods that handle noisy labels.
A noise mitigation method is named by both halves: what selects the molecules, and
what is then done to them. The group follows from the two.

     What selects the molecules
     Detector                 A score inferred from the features and the labels,
                              saying how wrong each label looks. The score is
                              that molecule’s suspicion score.
     Known flag               Already recorded in the data, with nothing inferred:
                              a label at the assay limit, how many times a molecule
                              was measured, which source reported it, which scaf-
                              fold group it belongs to.
     Every molecule           No selection. The method acts on all the labels.
     What is done to them
     Delete                   The molecule leaves the training set.
     Down-weight              The rows and labels are kept and each molecule car-
                              ries a weight.
     Move the label           The label shifts toward a target by some distance.
                              The target is a neighbors’ mean, a predicted value,
                              a group mean or an assay limit.
     The groups
     Noise detection       A detector alone, with nothing done afterwards.
     Noise mitigation      A detector, a known flag or every molecule, and then
                           one of the three actions.
     Noise-robust training Nothing is selected and the data is untouched. The
                           loss, the kernel, a noise term, the training loop or
                           the model changes.


   No single method is expected to win. Averaged over all problems, no learning
algorithm outperforms another [101]. Neighbor-based cleaning relies on the similar
property principle. It dates back to editing a training set by its nearest neighbors
[102]. However, activity cliffs break that principle, and QSAR models frequently fail
to predict cliffs [57]. Chapter 4 tests the principle, and Chapter 7 tests neighbor-based
noise mitigation with the adaptive hybrid representation as the feature space.
