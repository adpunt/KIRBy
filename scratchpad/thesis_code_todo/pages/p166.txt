   We found no published source for the rank scheme and count it as new. An earlier
comparison of six schemes on four datasets scored 48 pairs of dataset and representa-
tion. The rank scheme won just over half of them, 1/d2 most of the rest and equal
weights none. The winning margin was at most 0.0015 RMSE, below the spread across
folds; for this reason we sweep both.
   Importance weighting is feature-weighted nearest neighbors [249], modified so that
the weights come from the importance scores of the hybrid search. Each feature is
multiplied by its weight before the distance is computed, x′ = x ⊙ w, with negative
importances set to 0. For a single representation, the importance s is computed on the
fold’s training molecules, with LightGBM gain in most experiments. For a hybrid, it
is the importance its search used to select the features, with two exceptions. Distance
against label difference refits LightGBM gain on the hybrid’s features together, and
retrieval recomputes the search’s importance on the hybrid as a whole. We use two
formulas for w:

   • for distance against label difference and the best number of features, w = (s/s̄)a ,
     with a = 1 under Manhattan distance, a = 0.5 under cosine and a = 2 under
     Minkowski with p = 0.5;

   • for every other experiment that weights, w = s1/2 under every distance.

The exponent a of the first formula is set so that each feature’s term in the distance
is multiplied by its relative importance, sf /s̄, under all three distances. The second
formula has no such matching. Under Manhattan distance, a feature with four times
the average importance counts four times as much as an average feature under the
first formula, and only twice as much under the second. The weighted distances of
Sections 6.3.1 and 6.3.2 are therefore the same distance only under cosine. Some
experiments divide the second formula by a constant, which changes neither the ranking
of distances nor any prediction from neighbors. The square root was first written for
Euclidean distance and kept for the others, and no experiment in this chapter varies
the exponent. An earlier comparison on four datasets tested it against a power of 1
at every distance. The square root was better in 34 of 48 dataset and representation
pairs, and in 19 of 33 under Manhattan distance.
