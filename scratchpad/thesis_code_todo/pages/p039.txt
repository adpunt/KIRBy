   Studies involving noise, and specifically the quantification of noise robustness, typ-
ically approach label noise by adding independent, zero-mean, Gaussian error to the
labels. However, there’s evidence to suggest that noise from experimental assays takes
on different forms. Kruger and Overington [79] rejected normality of the differences
in binding affinity between orthologous targets by Anderson–Darling test, found them
approximately Laplace, and found that differences between assays of the same ligand
and target closely resemble them. Kalliokoski et al. [13] examined the widest disagree-
ments among 16,844 pairs of repeat pIC50 measurements and found that in most cases,
the wide gap between repeat values was due to annotation error rather than to an
imprecise assay.
   Although numerous studies have explored the effects of noise on ML algorithms for
molecular property prediction, comparisons between different molecular representations
remain limited. Kolmar and Grulke [10] examined the predictive limits of QSAR in the
presence of noise, showing that by learning the underlying trends rather than fitting
to noise, the models can achieve predictions more accurate than those of their training
data. They assumed experimental noise could be modeled as Gaussian-distributed,
using the Central Limit Theorem to argue that aggregated experimental measurements
from multiple independent error sources exhibit a Gaussian distribution. However, Heid
et al. [80] distinguish random noise, which affects all molecules alike, from systematic
noise that differs between regions of chemical space. Using synthetic noise applied
only to nitrogen-containing molecules, they showed that mean-variance estimation can
detect it.
   Beyond just the distribution, the assumptions of independence and zero mean may
fail as well [81]. Bentz et al. [82] examined P-glycoprotein transport measurements
from 23 laboratories and found that, among the laboratories using cell lines, 62% of
the variance in log digoxin efflux ratio was due to differences between laboratories.
When you pool data from different sources, each source has the potential to carry a
shared offset. These sources usually contain families of related compounds, breaking
the independence assumption. The zero-mean assumption is not assured either, espe-
cially when true values fall above or below an assay’s detection limit, called censoring.
Svensson et al. [83] count censored labels in 15 industrial assays, of which 13 carry cen-
sored labels and eight sit between a quarter and two thirds censored. Chapter 3 tests
noise conditions that vary the distribution, the correlation, and the bias of injected
error separately, at a matched quantity of noise for every condition except censoring.
