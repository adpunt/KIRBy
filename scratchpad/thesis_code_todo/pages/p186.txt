   Each dataset has two downstream models. In each outer fold, a search on that
fold’s training molecules ranks each model’s two best single representations and two
best hybrids. Every method is tested on all four. In most experiments that search
sees the clean training labels. Only the hybrid’s features are then re-ranked, and its
budget re-split between base representations, on the damaged labels; in this way the
hybrid’s features move with the damage. The base representations and the way the
hybrid is built, however, are chosen with clean labels, which a real user would not have.
There are two exceptions. The expanded published sweep repeats the search for every
damage kind, level and draw, trained and scored on the damaged labels alone, and
the experiment on cleaning before the feature search (Section 7.2.3) compares cleaning
before the search with cleaning after it.
   We keep apart three tasks that are often treated as one: finding the damaged labels,
sizing the damage, and improving the model’s held-out score. A method can succeed
at the first and fail at the third. Figure 7.1 shows how one method is run and scored
in one outer fold.


7.2.1    Noise conditions
We damage a regression dataset in six ways (Table 7.1). Four are noise conditions from
Section 2.5.1: Gaussian, outlier, grouped-shifted and censoring. Censoring clips labels
at a fixed value that imitates an assay’s reporting limit, the edge of its measurable
range. The clip is at the top of the label range. For potency labels such as hERG Ki ,
real censoring falls mostly at the low end; the clip imitates the rarer case. Under outlier
noise, a random 5% of molecules get an error three times wider, where Chapter 3 used
10%. Because every other label still gets the ordinary error, the 5% carry about a
third of the squared error, not all of it. The last two kinds raise a random 10% of the
training labels by one fixed amount, in the two units of Table 7.1. The grouped-wider
condition and the Student-t and Laplace shapes of Chapter 3 are not used here.
