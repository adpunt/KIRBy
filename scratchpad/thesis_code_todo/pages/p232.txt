   On small molecules, a user who builds a hybrid loses nothing by cleaning afterwards.
Whether a noise-handling method works depends on the feature space it runs in, as
Chapter 4 found for distance.


7.4        Conclusions
On the first outer fold, we found noisy labels in QSAR data and, in several settings,
corrected them. QM9, the one dataset with computed labels, gave the cleanest test
of whether a method removes noise or trains a better model (Section 7.3.2). Deletion
never helped QM9’s clean labels, and the published methods did not help it beyond
the network’s own spread. Where they helped an antibody dataset as delivered, they
found noise that was already in the data. Label smoothing and weighting improved
QM9 as well, as did changes to the model. Their gains on measured labels as delivered
are therefore largely better training, with label smoothing on hERG Ki the exception.
Corrections worked best with a flag: raising labels clipped at an assay limit won back
half to three quarters of the lost RMSE on the small-molecule datasets, with no detec-
tor. With a detector, deleting or relabeling flagged molecules helped under label flips
and outliers. These gains passed random and shuffled controls on hERG, hERG Ki ,
Buchwald–Hartwig and three antibody datasets, though QM9 shows that the shuffled
control alone is weak evidence with the network. The hybrid stayed ahead of the single
representation on small molecules in almost every condition, before and after treat-
ment. Finding a detector’s neighbors in the hybrid rather than in the model’s own
single representation helped slightly more often, with large differences in both direc-
tions. Which noise-handling method works best thus depends on the feature space it
runs in.
   Most results rest on the first outer fold; the further folds that exist mostly agree
with it, and the text gives the exceptions. The results to confirm first are, in order, the
cleaning of SMAC colloidal stability’s labels as delivered and relabeling and cliff put-
back on hERG Ki , then cleaning under hERG’s flips, the clipped-label corrections and
the scaffold-family offsets. Cleaning before the search and moving every label toward a
Huber-fitted prediction gave large gains but have no QM9 result with no noise added,
so their gains cannot yet be split into noise removed and a better model. Chapter 8
uses these results to answer the research questions of Chapter 1 and to set out how to
choose a noise-handling method for a new dataset.
