   The score uses the Manhattan distance of Chapter 4, on features scaled as above.
Each standardized feature adds a term of similar size to this distance. A represen-
tation’s weight in the distance, which the allocation method sets, is thus roughly its
number of features divided by the budget. A fingerprint weighs less than this per
feature, because a feature that only takes the values 0 and 1 adds less. The neighbor
score uses no importance weighting. We chose Manhattan distance in an earlier com-
parison of four distances on hERG Ki and LogD, over seven representations each. It
was the only distance never much worse than second on any of three scores: splitting
the budget, predicting from neighbors and agreeing with shared structure, although
cosine was better at predicting from neighbors. The inverse-distance weights were not
compared when the score was built. A later comparison on four datasets used Man-
hattan distance with 5 neighbors. There, inverse-distance weights beat equal weights
in all 48 pairs of dataset and representation, but lost to inverse squared distance in 39
of the 48.
   To rank whole candidates, we compute the score on the held-out molecules of each
fold of the search, inside the outer training fold. These molecules act as each other’s
neighbors. Their groups (scaffold families, for small molecules) are split into up to
5 parts, and each molecule’s 5 nearest neighbors are drawn from the other parts. A
neighbor from the same scaffold family is an easy match and would inflate the score.
This is the only place in the chapter where a molecule’s own group is kept out of its
neighbors. Each neighbor is weighted by the inverse of its distance [247]:

A neighbor at distance 0 receives all of the weight, shared equally if there are several.
On regression data, the score q5 is the Spearman correlation ρ(ŷ, y). On binary data,
ŷi is the distance-weighted fraction of neighbors in class 1, and q5 = 2(AUROC − 0.5),
where AUROC is the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. Both scores
put chance at 0, which the budget split needs, because each representation’s share of
the budget is its score divided by the sum of the scores. The modelability index of
Golbraikh et al. [126] (Chapter 4) is related, but uses a single nearest neighbor and a
hard class match.
