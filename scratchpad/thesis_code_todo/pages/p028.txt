2.1     Overview
Chapter 1 set out the questions this thesis asks; this chapter gives the background they
rest on. We first describe the molecular representations and models we use. We then
cover molecular similarity and activity cliffs. Label noise follows, with its sources in
bioactivity data and the ways we inject it, and then the methods for learning with noisy
labels. The chapter closes with our NoiseInject package and how results are evaluated.


2.2     Molecular representations in QSAR
The success of machine learning hinges on the quality of input data. Although the
quality can refer to noise, which the majority of our work is centered around, in this
case we are talking about the type of input. Molecular representations provide differ-
ent features with which QSAR models and noise detection techniques can be learned.
However, a potential bottleneck on larger datasets should be noted; the more com-
plex the data structure, the greater the computational cost. For smaller datasets, the
number of parameters is limited by the size of the training set, to prevent overfitting.
   One of the most common and intuitive molecular representations is the Simplified
Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES) string, which consists of a sequence of
ASCII characters generated by a fixed set of rules to specify a given chemical identity
[20]. A common way to use SMILES strings as inputs to QSAR models is to tokenize
and one-hot-encode them rather than to use the strings themselves [21]. The strings are
