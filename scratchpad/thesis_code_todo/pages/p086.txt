features show little to no variance. Some representations assign separate molecules
identical feature vectors. Since common fingerprints do not encode stereochemistry
[124], they are often unable to differentiate stereoisomers, which can have substantially
different measured activity [125]. As these coefficients compare every feature, irrelevant
features dilute the result. Different representations also disagree about which molecules
are close based on the information they carry.
   The problem of whether neighboring molecules can predict chemical activity has
been measured before. The modelability index of Golbraikh et al. [126] scores a binary
dataset by a class-weighted share of nearest-neighbor pairs that share an activity class,
producing a single value for how strongly the similarity assumption holds on that par-
ticular combination of representation and data. It is not clear whether a low value is
due to the data being intrinsically hard to model or to the representation not capturing
adequate features for the task. Other work in this area relates to activity cliffs. Sheri-
dan et al. [127] compared experimental error, activity distribution and activity-cliff
metrics as predictors of how well a dataset can be modeled, and found the cliff metrics
the strongest of the three. However, they found that these metrics cannot separate real
cliffs from those produced by uncertain activity measurements. Medina-Franco [128]
point out that some of these activity cliffs are artifacts of the chosen representation,
similarity threshold, or noise in the data. Activity cliffs are distinct from noise, as
they carry some of the most valuable information about the interaction between struc-
ture and activity. None of these measures ask specifically about whether the specific
features within these representations matter for the property at hand.
   We first consider how much the representation impacts similarity measures. Riniker
and Landrum [129] benchmarked 12 fingerprints and two simple baseline fingerprints
for similarity-based virtual screening on 88 targets from three public collections, and
found that the screening performance of the 12 fingerprints was highly correlated.
Only the two baselines differed significantly from the rest. Nearly all of 25 pretrained
neural embeddings were found to have a negligible improvement over ECFP across
25 datasets [130]. However, when looking into the differences between the structure
of fingerprints, Huber and Pollmann [131] found that fingerprints which store raw
counts are more effective at defining similarity than folded fingerprints when compared
against a graph-based structural reference. Folding introduces bit collisions, which
disproportionately distort similarity for molecules with many set bits [131]. ACs follow
the same pattern: across 15 configurations of representation and similarity measures on
3 datasets, the representations disagreed about which pairs form cliffs [132], supporting
the point Medina-Franco [128] make about ACs being artifacts of their representation.
