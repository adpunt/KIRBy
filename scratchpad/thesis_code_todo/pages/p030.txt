   Neural fingerprints are a category of learned representations that overcome the
fixed, hard-coded nature of traditional fingerprints. Unlike ECFP or MACCS keys,
which rely on predefined hashing schemes or substructure catalogs, neural fingerprints
train on molecular data to capture chemical information [25, 26]. These representa-
tions are the result of deep learning architectures that have learned dense, continuous
embeddings which can be used for downstream tasks.
   Mol2vec, which adapts the Word2vec algorithm from NLP to molecular substruc-
tures [25], is a common neural fingerprint. Mol2vec treats molecules like “sentences”
and circular substructures derived from Morgan fingerprints like “words”. The model
learns vector embeddings for each substructure through unsupervised training, such
that chemically similar substructures are mapped to nearby points in the embedding
space [25]. The embeddings of all present substructures are summed to obtain a full
representation. This approach produces 300-dimensional dense vectors that avoid the
sparsity and bit collisions inherent in hashed fingerprints [25]. Mol2vec has performed
strongly on various QSAR benchmarks, matching or exceeding Morgan fingerprints on
solubility and bioactivity prediction tasks [25].
   Transformer-based architectures have also been applied to molecular representation
learning, similarly stemming from NLP ideas. MolBERT applies a bidirectional trans-
former to learn molecular representations from SMILES strings through self-supervised
pre-training [27]. The model has domain-relevant auxiliary adaptations, such as the
prediction of calculated physicochemical properties alongside the masked token pre-
diction, to improve the chemical fidelity of the learned embeddings [27]. MolBERT
outperformed count-based ECFP4 (ECFC4), RDKit descriptors and the CDDD neu-
ral representation on virtual screening and QSAR benchmarks [27]. ChemBERTa
is another transformer-based approach which pre-trains masked language models on
SMILES data from PubChem [26]. ChemBERTa uses a byte-pair-encoding tokenizer
by default and compares it with a SMILES-specific tokenizer [26]. Attention visual-
ization shows neurons selective for chemically relevant functional groups and aromatic
rings, and others that track bracket closures in the SMILES string [26]. ChemBERTa-
2, pre-trained on up to 77 million PubChem compounds, is competitive with existing
architectures on MoleculeNet [28].
