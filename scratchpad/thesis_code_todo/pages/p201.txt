7.3.2    The clean-label test: where noise is removed
QM9 separates the two things a gain can mean (Section 7.2.5). A method that re-
moves noise should leave QM9’s computed labels alone and improve measured labels
as delivered. A method that also helps QM9 is, at least in part, a better way to train.
We apply this test to each kind of method here, before the sections that go through
them one by one.
   Deletion passes the test. On QM9 with no noise added it made every model worse,
in every configuration (Table 7.11). Deleting 20% of the molecules flagged by the forest
residual does the same on QM9’s labels (Table 7.12). On SMAC colloidal stability and
expression titer, the same deletion improved the labels as delivered and beat every
shuffled control. These are the clearest cases in the chapter of a method finding noise
that was already in the data. On the small-molecule labels as delivered, deletion found
nothing to remove (Section 7.3.3).
   Label smoothing, weighting and moving labels toward their neighbors pass the test
only in part. With the network they improve QM9 with no noise added (Tables 7.10
and 7.12, and the QM9 row of Figure 7.3). LightGBM, which gives the same score on
every fit, barely moves on QM9. On Caco-2 and LogD as delivered, the network gains
about as much as it does on QM9, so those gains are not evidence of noise. The support
vector regressor, which also gives the same score on every fit, gains almost nothing from
any cleanup on these labels. Label smoothing on hERG Ki is the exception among the
small molecules. Relative to its score, the network’s first single representation gains
several times what the same cleanup gives on QM9 (Table 7.12).
   QM9 also shows the limit of the shuffled-score control. With the network, label
smoothing on QM9’s first single representation beats all three shuffles, although there
is no noise to find (Table 7.12). The shuffles themselves move about as much as the
network does between fits. Passing the shuffled control with the network is therefore
weak evidence by itself, and we read it beside QM9.
   Changing the model fails the test, so its gains are better training more than noise
handling. On QM9 with no noise added, stacking the two models improves every
configuration, and averaging network seeds improves most of them (Table 7.30). On
the labels as delivered of hERG Ki and LogD the same changes help by about as much.
Under injected Gaussian noise, seed averaging on hERG Ki gains more than on the
labels as delivered, in every configuration (Table 7.29). Part of its gain under damage
does respond to the noise.
