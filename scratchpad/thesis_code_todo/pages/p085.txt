Limitations of the similarity
assumption

4.1     Introduction
The previous chapters have been focused on the impact of label noise. Before we delve
into techniques that are meant to train on, identify, and mitigate such label noise, we
first focus on a tangential point, molecular similarity. All of the aforementioned noise-
adjacent methods, as well as the QSAR modeling itself, rest on the similar property
principle (SPP; Section 2.4), which posits that structurally similar compounds display
similar chemical properties [73]. This serves as the conceptual foundation for QSAR
modeling. The concept of molecular similarity is also used in various contexts to
compare neighboring molecules. This comes into play when identifying noisy molecules
that in theory stick out from their neighbors. There are some important exceptions
to the SPP, namely activity cliffs (ACs) [58, 77]. Furthermore, there is no single way
to define similarity between compounds. This is where the principle begins to break
down in practice. When defining similarity between two molecules, we typically rely
on some pairwise mathematical function, or coefficient, that takes in two molecular
representations as input. Two factors vary here, the representation and the coefficient.
Bajusz et al. [75] compared eight coefficients and found Tanimoto, Dice, Cosine and
Soergel the best and largely equivalent, while similarities derived from Euclidean and
Manhattan distances were not recommended on their own. In this chapter we ask if
the standard methods of comparing molecules satisfy the SPP, insofar as they do not
sufficiently quantify how similar two molecules actually are.
