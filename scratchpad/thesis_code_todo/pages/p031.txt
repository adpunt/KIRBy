then fine-tuned for specific downstream tasks, opening the door to transfer learning
[26, 27]. However, pre-training them is computationally expensive, and they are less
directly interpretable than fingerprints, although attention visualization offers some
insight [26].
    Beyond fingerprints, physicochemical-descriptor vectors (PDVs) are a class of
continuous-value molecular descriptors that encode structural and chemical properties
of molecules [29]. Unlike binary fingerprints or graph-based representations, PDVs
aggregate atom and bond-level quantities into fixed-length feature vectors that
capture a wide variety of information about a molecule and its structure [30]. PDVs
typically consist of hundreds of properties including constitutional descriptors such as
molecular weight, atom and bond counts, and heteroatom ratios; topological indices
encoding molecular connectivity patterns; physicochemical properties such as logP
(lipophilicity), molar refractivity, and topological polar surface area; and electronic
descriptors derived from partial charges and polarizabilities [29, 31].      The RDKit
cheminformatics toolkit provides a comprehensive suite of more than 200 molecular
descriptors that cover these categories [32]. Many PDV descriptors, such as logP,
molecular weight and partial charges, have a direct physicochemical meaning, although
many of the thousands available have no clear interpretation [31]. They are also less
computationally expensive than some 3D methods or neural fingerprints, with many
descriptors simply requiring a 2D molecular structure without conformer generation
or extensive pre-training. This efficiency makes PDVs particularly appropriate for
high-throughput applications. Descriptors based on 3D structure can also be used
[30].
    However, PDVs have limitations. The dimensionality of PDV feature spaces can
lead to problems with multicollinearity [29]. This can be mitigated by feature selection
or dimensionality reduction techniques [33, 34]. Additionally, PDVs encode molecular
properties as aggregated global features, which may not capture fine-grained structural
details such as specific substructure arrangements or local chemical environments, in-
formation important for particular endpoints.

encode topological atom- and bond-level features but leave out geometric properties
such as bond angles or torsion, meaning they may fail to capture stereochemistry. 3D
graphs incorporate these spatial features, but are often limited by remaining in a single
conformation [35]. A standard molecular graph is constructed such that each molecule
