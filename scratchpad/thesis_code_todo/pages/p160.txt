   The fourth question asked whether weighting features by their importance improves
the hybrid and model training. In construction, ranking features by importance within
each representation is what makes the hybrid work. In training, by contrast, using
the importance scores again adds little on small molecules and helps on some small
antibody datasets, with no condition that works everywhere.
   The hybrid needs data. Its gains are consistent on datasets of a few thousand
samples and inconsistent on a few hundred, where the search’s own scores overstate
what the hybrid delivers (Section 5.3.1). Most of our antibody datasets are that small,
so their results say as much about dataset size as about antibodies. The antibody pools
also offer few kinds of representation, mostly language models of the same sequence.
The search is costly and must be rerun when the data, representations or model change.
Where a simpler selection, such as recursive elimination or a tuned model on a wider
hybrid, does as well, the search costs more than it returns. The separate sites are
simulated from one public dataset.
   This chapter used the hybrid only as input to a model. Whether hybrids keep
more of their performance under label noise, and whether their features stabilize noise
mitigation, are the subjects of Chapters 6 and 7. Chapter 6 first asks whether a feature
set chosen for the task also gives better neighbors, which every method that relies on
similarity could use, including the detection of noisy labels.
