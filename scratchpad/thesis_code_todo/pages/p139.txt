   Table 5.14 gives the antibody results, which differ from one dataset to the next. On
the anti-VEGF expression scan, the largest antibody set, the hybrid is ahead with the
SVM on all five folds. On ∆∆G the mean gain is the largest in the chapter with both
models, though the gain moves by more than its own size from fold to fold. On EGFR
binding, expression titer and AC-SINS self-association the hybrid’s mean is higher with
both models, while it wins on only two to four of the five folds.
   In an antibody representation, as in a molecular fingerprint, the features that matter
are few and buried among many that carry nothing: a language model’s embedding
of the whole sequence mixes the CDRs that vary with a framework that mostly does
not. Many of the antibody representations in the pools were built for this thesis to
separate the two (Table 5.11). On four antibody datasets the hybrid beats even the
single representation that was best in hindsight, and on two of them that representation
is one of ours (Table 5.14). On the anti-VEGF expression scan it is the CDR readout
of ESM3 with the SVM, and on ∆∆G it is the mutation features with the SVM and
the change from the parent embedding with the DNN.
   In the ∆∆G hybrid, the representations built for this thesis carry most of the
drop in score under shuffling with both models. On the other antibody datasets they
carry much less, so they matter most where the antibodies differ by defined mutations.
On AC-SINS self-association, another of the four, the hybrid draws mainly on two
antibody language models, IgT5 and AbLang-2. Across the antibody datasets the
hybrids draw on about one and a half to five representations by the same shuffling
(Additional file 24). On angiopoietin-2 binding the best representation in hindsight
with the SVM, AntiBERTy, was never ranked first on any fold’s training antibodies.
