pooled from several laboratories can carry an offset per source, and an assay with a
reporting limit gives clipped labels; both come with a flag that a correction can use.
A method that assumes noise that is not there can do harm. Simple methods were
often as good as complex ones: random forests were the most consistent model, and
on hERG Ki one constant lift for every clipped label beat censored EM.


8.3     Contributions
We make six contributions on label noise and three on the representation, in that order.

  1. NoiseInject, an open-source Python package for benchmarking noise robustness
      and uncertainty in QSAR models (Section 2.6.1).

  2. Noise conditions beyond Gaussian noise, including noise shared within scaffold
      families and labels clipped at an assay limit (Chapter 2).

  3. A split of the variance in performance and in noise robustness between model
      family and representation (Chapter 3).

  4. Published noise-handling methods adapted to QSAR data, for example censored
      EM with any downstream model, a random intercept per scaffold family, and the
      buddy check from meteorology as a detector of noisy labels (Chapter 7).

  5. Three methods for which we found no earlier use: the cliff-aware gap, cleaning
      the labels before the hybrid search, and returning activity-cliff molecules after a
      cleanup (Chapter 7).

  6. Tests of these methods on single representations and hybrids side by side, with
      three controls: QM9 with no noise added, random deletion and shuffled detector
      scores (Chapter 7).

  7. The adaptive hybrid representation, which ranks features within each representa-
      tion, splits a budget of features among them, and searches the selection settings
      for each dataset and model (Chapter 5). A newly published embedding is added
      to the search as one more representation.

  8. Hybrids built for distance (Chapter 6), and hybrids built from data held at sep-
      arate sites (Chapter 5).
