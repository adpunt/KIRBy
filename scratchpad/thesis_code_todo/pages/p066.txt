   While alternative shapes of noise may be accurately modeled by the Gaussian dis-
tribution, we do not find that is the case for systematic label noise. Relative to Gaus-
sian, grouped-shifted noise lowered the AUCnorm across all models and representations
(Table 3.3). The models struggle to make predictions when the noise is isolated on
molecules of specific scaffold families instead of being applied randomly across every
sample. The variational networks retained the most predictive performance, while the
plain, deterministic NNs lost the most. The predictive abilities of BNN-α degraded at
a slower rate compared to its deterministic counterpart on every representation except
PDV, while BNN-β did so only on MHG-GNN and PDV. Grouped-wider noise changed
no model beyond its replicate spread except QRF on Avalon and MHG-GNN, which
makes predictions particularly well under this noise condition. Censoring created the
largest drop in predictive performance, AUCnorm fell by 0.12 to 0.18 for the same QSAR
models relative to Gaussian noise (Table 3.2).
   Most of these observations are being made at the upper end of the noise range.
With ECFP4, six of the seven most robust models start within 0.015 of one another
in R2 for clean labels, stay together up to a noise level of 0.5 and separate only above
1.0, finishing between 0.692 for LightGBM and 0.741 for RF (Figure 3.3). We ranked
the models by predictive performance at each noise level. Of the 13 base models, RF
started ranked seventh, but by level 1.0 it was ranked first. SVM on the other hand
falls from first to fifth (Figure 3.8). Because AUCnorm weights the whole range from 0
to 1.5 equally, and the first half of it separates little, it has the potential to understate
just how much the pattern of the noise costs. However, choosing to look at performance
of a set level has its drawbacks, as the ranks shift at a different pace between noise
conditions.
