bels. The task of trying to determine if a molecule’s value matches that of its neighbors
becomes a lot harder when all of the neighbors have some form of noise as well.
   There are many downstream tasks which rely on the concept of similarity. The
second half of this thesis relies on many such tasks, and as such focuses on identifying
representations that agree with this principle.
   An activity cliff is a pair of structurally similar molecules whose potency differs
by a large amount because of a small chemical change [76, 77]. Whether a pair is
a cliff depends on the similarity measure, and there is no widely accepted definition
[58]. The MoleculeACE benchmark [58] uses three measures: the Tanimoto coefficient
on ECFPs, the Tanimoto coefficient on scaffold fingerprints and a scaled Levenshtein
distance on SMILES strings. A pair forms a cliff if its similarity exceeds 90% under
at least one of them and the two molecules differ tenfold or more in Ki or EC50 [58].
Across the thirty MoleculeACE targets, molecules in at least one cliff pair make up
between 7% and 52% of a dataset [58].
   As the number of dimensions grows, the distances to the nearest and to the furthest
point become increasingly similar [78]. Molecular representations are routinely high-
dimensional and sparse. When many of their features carry no information about the
property, distances computed over them are dominated by those features.


2.5     Noise in molecular data
Real-world cheminformatics datasets contain label noise arising from multiple sources
across the drug discovery pipeline. Although noise is often modeled as being random,
or drawn from a Gaussian distribution, other varieties of noise must be considered when
working with experimental data. To properly model experimental noise, we must first
understand where the noise is coming from.
   One key issue with QSAR models is the standard ML assumption that labels rep-
resent true values, as most chemical datasets lack sufficient replications to provide a
robust statistical representation of the underlying distributions [10]. A single mea-
surement, or even multiple measurements for a given label, will not guarantee that
this label accurately reflects the population mean [10]. Most ML models rely on the
assumption that the training data is, in fact, an accurate reflection of the population.

distributions, which opens the door to overfitting to noise. However, Bayesian meth-
