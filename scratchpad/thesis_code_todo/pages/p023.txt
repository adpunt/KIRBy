flawed if test and validation sets are laden with noise. Kolmar and Grulke [10] showed
that error in the test labels can make a model look worse than it is: a model trained
on noisy data can predict the noise-free values more accurately than an evaluation on
the noisy test set indicates. There is also a limit to how much noise the model can
tolerate; Jorner et al. [11] argued that once their model’s error approached the exper-
imental error, more training data would not be expected to improve accuracy much.
In the context of activity prediction, the inherent variability within experimental se-
tups and assays, batch effects, measurement errors, and biological variability introduce
additional layers of complexity [10, 12, 13]. All of this increases the likelihood of en-
countering noise in the data. Even the molecular representations in the training data
themselves may be incorrect or misleading [14, 15], and chemical datasets contain too
few repeat measurements of each compound to characterize the distribution the label is
drawn from. Thus, models treat a single label as the population mean [10]. Here pIC50
and pKi are the negative base-10 logarithms of the IC50 and Ki in molar units, so a
difference of one log unit is a tenfold difference in concentration. Repeat measurements
of the same compound-target pair have a standard deviation of about 0.68 log units
for pIC50 and 0.54 log units for pKi [12, 13], which is about a 4.8-fold and a 3.5-fold
difference in concentration. Restricting to measurements from a single assay lowers
the mean disagreement from 0.50 to 0.27 log units [16], or from about a 3.2-fold to a
1.9-fold difference.
   This thesis addresses the challenge of conducting molecular property prediction
when the underlying experimental data, or “labels” are noisy. Most work on noise in
QSAR modeling assumes Gaussian label noise and evaluates either representations or
model architectures in isolation. Cortes-Ciriano et al. [17] examined 12 algorithms
across multiple noise levels but used fixed representations, and studies that compare
representations typically use clean data. No work has systematically compared the
relative importance of representation versus architecture choice for label noise robust-
ness, asked whether probabilistic methods justify their computational overhead under
noise, or tested whether findings generalize across different types of injected noise.
This thesis fills that gap. I will also discuss noise mitigation, using the results of my
noise robustness study. I explore why many noise mitigation techniques underperform
in cheminformatics data and methods to address this problem in the form of novel
molecular representations.
