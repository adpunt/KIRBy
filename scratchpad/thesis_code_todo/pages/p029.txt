finally converted into binary vectors using one-hot encodings [21]. This representation,
referred to as one-hot-encoded SMILES, allows models to process molecular structures
as bit-vector-based fingerprints or as sequences of vectors. Sequence-based models such
as recurrent neural networks and transformers have proven to be particularly successful
in capturing text semantics within a sequence. When applied to raw or one-hot-encoded
SMILES strings, these models can effectively capture the structure of the molecule in
its textual representation [21].
   Another common representation is the molecular fingerprint, a vector containing
information about the properties of the molecule in question. In a typical binary finger-
print, each bit accounts for the presence or absence of a given chemical feature [22], and
in the case of Morgan fingerprints, a given substructure. One common fingerprint is
the Molecular ACCess System (MACCS) key, a substructure key-based fingerprint that
encodes predefined atom symbols and properties, as well as bond types [23]. Another is
the Extended Connectivity Fingerprint (ECFP), or Morgan fingerprint, which encodes
local neighborhoods around each atom and bond connectivity in molecules [23]. These
existing molecular representations are well established and have been quite success-
ful when coupled with QSAR models [4, 24]. They are relatively simple, and as the
bits can be mapped back to one or more substructures, they are human-interpretable,
and furthermore easy-to-compute. However, hashed fingerprints are subject to infor-
mation loss due to bit collisions, which happens when two different substructures are
encoded at the same bit position [22]. They are also based on a fixed procedure and
are not trainable or adaptable to a particular dataset. Alternative representations that
capture different structural, chemical, or physical features are able to overcome these
limitations.
   Recent advances have led to the development of new molecular fingerprints. One
such method is the Sort & Slice (SNS) technique, an alternative to hash-based fold-
ing for pooling ECFP substructures [7]. Sort & Slice begins by sorting all circular
substructures in the training set according to their frequency of occurrence. It then
“slices” away all but the N most frequent substructures, which are subsequently used to
generate an N -dimensional, bit-collision-free binary fingerprint. This approach solves
the ambiguity problem of hashed fingerprints, where multiple substructures might be
encoded at the same bit position, making it very interpretable [7]. Sort & Slice fin-
gerprints outperform ECFP of diameter four (ECFP4) with standard hash-based fold-
ing methods across various molecular property prediction tasks. These experiments
