depended on the representation they were given, as did the predictive performance of
NGBoost. Across datasets and noise conditions, RF and QRF were reliably robust,
although their predictive performance on clean labels was middling and on Caco-2 they
struggled under Gaussian noise. QRF was the slightly less robust of the two, falling
below RF under Gaussian and grouped-shifted noise. Other models performed well
only under some conditions. The GP had the highest predictive performance on clean
labels and under independent noise but fell behind when the noise was shared within a
scaffold family, NGBoost predicted well only with the right representation, and SVM
led in predictive performance under scaffold-shared noise on some datasets. Transform-
ing a feed-forward network into a Bayesian one increased AUCnorm , but the Bayesian
variants never predicted better at any noise level, so switching to a tree-based model
is the stronger move under label noise.
   With regard to uncertainty estimation, mean predicted uncertainty rose with label
noise for almost every probabilistic model on QM9 and for the majority of models on
the assay datasets. Of the models whose aleatoric and epistemic components can be
split per molecule, the BNNs with a variance head, the heteroscedastic GP and the
heteroscedastic VBLL networks correctly attributed error from the injected noise as
aleatoric, while QRF read that error as epistemic. The success of a split is also depen-
dent on the representation, which is not necessarily the case for predictive performance
or noise robustness. The aleatoric component rises by roughly 0.4 to 1 unit for each
unit of noise added, so it can compare noise between datasets or between noise levels,
but it does not measure a dataset’s internal error. Outside censoring, no model’s per-
sample uncertainty identified which labels had been corrupted. Under censoring the
models’ most confident predictions are the clipped labels themselves, and dividing a
molecule’s error by its uncertainty picked out the clipped labels better than the error
alone.
   QM9 served as a relatively clean baseline as its properties are computed. The
assay datasets containing experimental measurements on the endpoints LogD, Caco-2
[110], and hERG Ki [111] are smaller and cover a more narrow range of the chemical
space. As the labels in these data are experimentally-derived and pooled from different
laboratories, they contain some unknown amount of noise. We are then adding artificial
noise on top of the existing experimental noise. These validation datasets supported
the QM9 findings for the forests, with the boosted trees XGBoost and LightGBM the
notable exceptions, degrading on the external datasets. Future work should extend this
type of noise robustness benchmarking to classification tasks and larger experimental
datasets with better-known uncertainty estimates.
