perceptron (MLP), an NN containing fully-connected layers with non-linear activation
functions which transforms molecular feature vectors (i.e., fingerprints, descriptors)
into a property or activity prediction [47].
   A typical MLP for molecular property prediction has a few fully connected hid-
den layers with rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations and dropout regularization to
prevent overfitting. Training is performed using backpropagation with gradient de-
scent optimizers such as Adam, to minimize a loss function (mean squared error for
regression, cross-entropy for classification) on batches of training data.
   However, NN performance on QSAR-related tasks has been very mixed. NNs have
a long history in QSAR [48], and tuned deep networks have outperformed shallow
methods such as random forests and support vector machines across diverse activity
classes [49]. Their complexity and use of gradients make them prone to label mem-
orization [50], which in the context of noise is problematic, as it means that models
may memorize the noise themselves. Although models that are memorizing labels
may initially appear to have strong predictive performance, they cannot generalize
to out-of-distribution samples properly and often provide inaccurate uncertainty esti-
mates. Various approaches have been proposed to improve neural network robustness
to label noise, including noise-robust loss functions [51], confident learning to identify
mislabeled examples [52], and regularization techniques such as mixup [53] or label
smoothing [54].
   All the models up to this point have assumed a vector-based input. Molecular
graphs, on the other hand, require different types of predictive modeling. Two stan-
dard ML methods on molecular graphs include Graph Convolutional Networks (GCNs)
and Graph Isomorphism Networks (GINs). GCNs apply the concept of convolution to
graph-structured data. Instead of operating on grid-based data, such as images, GCNs
aggregate information from neighbors of a node to update its representation [55]. In
contrast, GINs address the limitations in GCNs’ ability to differentiate between non-
isomorphic graphs. Unlike GCNs, which normalize and weight the sum of neighboring
node features, GINs use a simple sum followed by a trainable MLP, giving the model
flexibility to learn complex transformations of node features. The GIN model’s de-
sign is informed by the Weisfeiler–Lehman test for graph isomorphism, enabling it to
distinguish between molecular graphs that are structurally similar but differ in subtle
