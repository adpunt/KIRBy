values, creating a tree where internal nodes represent decision rules, branches represent
the results of those decisions, and leaf nodes contain predicted values or class labels [43].
Each recursive split is chosen to maximize homogeneity within the resulting partitions.
For classification, Gini impurity is commonly used and for regression, mean squared
error reduction [43].
   There are several properties that make decision trees a good foundation for QSAR
modeling. First, they handle non-linear relationships between molecular descriptors
and target properties without requiring explicit feature engineering or transformation
[43]. They also do not make assumptions about the underlying distribution of the
data, making them robust to violations of parametric assumptions that can limit linear
regression or other classical methods [43].
   Decision trees handle data in a “contributory” rather than “consensus” manner.
In consensus methods such as linear regression or neural networks, every data point
influences the prediction at every other point in the feature space. Tree-based methods
make predictions based on the subset of training samples that fall into the same leaf
node as the test point [43]. This mechanism essentially quarantines outliers in their
respective leaf nodes, limiting their impact on predictions for samples in other regions
or chemical domains.
   Individual decision trees tend to have high variance and often overfit, particularly
at large depths [43]. Small modifications made to the training data can result in large
differences in the structure of the tree, resulting in unstable predictions [44]. Ensemble
methods are used to counteract this instability, aggregating predictions from many
trees. Random forests (RFs) aggregate predictions from trees trained on bootstrap
samples of data with random subsets of features at each split [45]. Bootstrapping
ensures that each tree is trained on a slightly different subset of data, which effectively
reduces the influence of any individual sample [45]. This is especially beneficial with
noisy samples. The selection of random features at every node decorrelates the trees,
which lowers the generalization error of the ensemble [45]. The aggregation of trees
further prevents overfitting to noise by smoothing out prediction errors from individual
noisy labels.
