   When training on noisy experimental data in the context of QSAR, it is important
to keep in mind that a model’s robustness to noise is primarily driven by its own
mechanism of training, and to an extent the molecular representation the input data
comes in, oftentimes with respect to the model. Of the models tested, RF was the most
consistent choice across the four datasets, ranking between first and eighth for AUCnorm
on every dataset, representation and noise condition. Where a predictive distribution is
wanted, its probabilistic counterpart QRF maintains RF’s predictive performance and
nearly all of its noise robustness. What QRF cannot do is say where that uncertainty
came from. Beyond the forests, the better choice depends on how much noise there is.
LightGBM and XGBoost predict well on clean labels and lose the most as noise rises,
so they suit data you trust, while NGBoost and the VBLL networks start lower and
hold what they have.
   In this research, we define a noise level as a fraction of the spread of the clean train-
ing labels. It is therefore relative to the dataset rather than an absolute measurement
error, and cannot be mapped onto a given dataset exactly. There are other mecha-
nisms used to estimate the noise in a dataset directly. When an assay’s controls are
available, the Z′ -factor can be used to estimate noise [86]. You can also use screening
data to test for row, column and plate effects, which produce systematic rather than
independent error [122]. When you lack controls, replicates, and metadata, a label can
be checked against the data itself. Confident learning flags labels by comparing each
label to what a model predicts for that molecule when it was left out of training [52].
You can use the point at which the model error matches the data’s error to generate a
predictive limit [10, 123]. Beyond formal methods, you should consider how a dataset
was assembled. How many independent replicates were done, how many laboratories
contributed, whether assay protocols were consistently applied, and how varied are the
compounds in chemical space?
   The first limitation of this study is data availability. While HOMO–LUMO gap from
QM9 is a good baseline as its computed labels carry no measurement error, it is only a
single task. As the noise is meant to replicate experimental data, this analysis would be
improved by more quality-controlled, relatively clean and well-replicated experimental
data across different targets. However, data of that nature are hard to find publicly.
