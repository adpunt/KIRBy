Abstract

This thesis investigates label noise in quantitative structure–activity relationship
(QSAR) models, whose labels carry measurement error, differences between laborato-
ries and assay limits. Most studies of label noise in QSAR assume Gaussian noise and
study representations or models in isolation. We show that the kind of noise matters,
and that noise can be handled once the features suit the data.
   First, we introduced NoiseInject, an open-source package for benchmarking noise
robustness. Across 13 base models, six representations and seven types of label noise,
model family decided robustness more than the representation did; random forests were
the most consistent model. Systematic noise, such as labels clipped at an assay limit,
did the most damage. Predicted uncertainty rose with noise but did not identify the
corrupted labels.
   Next, we traced why published methods for learning with noisy labels did poorly
on molecular data. Within one dataset, most features of a standard representation
do not vary or do not relate to the label, yet each still adds to the distance between
molecules. Methods built on that distance therefore inherit the problem.
   We then built the adaptive hybrid representation, which selects features from sev-
eral representations for each dataset and model. On every small-molecule dataset it
predicted better than the best single representation, and its distances tracked activity
more closely. A newly published embedding can be added as one more representation.
   Last, we returned to label noise with single representations and hybrids side by side.
Noise was handled, mostly by methods built or adapted for molecular data. Which
method worked depended on the task, the data, the model and the noise, but on small
molecules the hybrid stayed ahead through every treatment.
   The noise in a molecular dataset should therefore be modeled before a method is
chosen, and the features adapted to each dataset.
