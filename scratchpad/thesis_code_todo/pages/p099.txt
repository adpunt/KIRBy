Noise detection by neighbors

Neighbor-based noise detection treats a label far from its neighbors’ labels as prob-
ably wrong, an idea that goes back to editing nearest-neighbor training sets [102].
The wider literature on filtering noisy labels concerns classification [155, 156], but
Martín et al. [157] adapt fourteen classification noise filters to regression, seven of
them nearest-neighbor filters that use the neighbors’ mean label. They find that the
filters improve regression methods on 20 general regression benchmarks with injected
label noise, none of them QSAR datasets. The neighbor-based noise scores, sample
removal, sample weighting, label smoothing and the recovery rate are defined in Chap-
ter 7 (Sections 7.2.2 and 7.2.3, Equation 7.6).
   The method needs a distance that picks the right neighbors, and neighbors with
clean labels. Earlier sections of this chapter argue that the first condition fails, and
the second fails whenever noise is spread over the whole training set. A large gap from
the neighbors’ labels can mean a noisy label, a noisy neighbor or the wrong neighbors,
and the score cannot tell which. Activity cliffs also look like noise to such a detector
(Chapter 2, “Molecular similarity and activity cliffs”).
   The exploratory study also used neighbors to clean labels, on 5,000 randomly chosen
QM9 molecules (HOMO–LUMO gap, scaffold split) with Gaussian noise on the training
labels at σ = 0.3 and σ = 0.6. Each cleaning method was judged by retraining a random
forest on ECFP4 with clean, noisy and cleaned labels, giving the recovery rate, the share
of the R2 lost to noise that cleaning recovers (Equation 7.6). Neighbor-based scores
compared each label with those of the molecule’s k nearest training molecules under
Euclidean, Manhattan or cosine distance. Table 4.4 holds only the configurations built
on a single representation. This study is separate from the sample-removal study of
Chapter 7.
   With the neighbor-based scores used as sample weights at strength α = 0.1, mean
recovery was negative for every representation at both noise levels (Table 4.4). Of all
860 cleaning configurations, 8 had positive recovery at both noise levels; 6 of these
used a single representation, and 4 of those 6 were neighbor-based. Label smoothing
toward neighbor or model estimates gave positive recovery in 5.0% of configurations
at σ = 0.3 and 0.8% at σ = 0.6. Mean recovery was negative at every weighting and
smoothing strength tried, from 0.1 to 0.7.
