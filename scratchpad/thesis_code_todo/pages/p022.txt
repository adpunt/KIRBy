Chapter 1

Introduction

New avenues in the drug discovery pipeline have opened up thanks to advances in large
dataset collection, analysis, and computation. Therapeutic targets are identified, po-
tential drug candidates are screened and optimized, and the most promising of these go
on to pre-clinical and clinical trials [1]. However, attrition is high, and roughly 90% of
drugs that enter clinical trials are never approved, often due to poor efficacy or toxicity
[2]. Computational methods can remove poor candidates early on in the drug discovery
pipeline, long before clinical trials. This is done through virtual screening, which allows
for a large-scale analysis of libraries of compounds [3]. One standard screening method
is the prediction of bioactivities and pharmacological properties of such candidates.
There is a long history of using Quantitative Structure-Activity Relationship (QSAR)
models to make these predictions, to act as a filter for compounds with undesirable
properties, and thus reduce the time and cost associated with experimental screening
[4]. QSAR models aim to predict the relationship between a molecule’s structural and
chemical features as encoded by a particular molecular representation and a desired
experimental molecular property [4] of interest. A QSAR model requires a molecular
representation and some form of machine learning (ML) model. An ideal molecular
representation will allow a model to learn only the relevant information about each
molecule with respect to the target property. Representations are either ‘static’ hand-
crafted descriptors or circular fingerprints that encode structural and physicochemical
features [5–8]; or learned representations, where the encoding is derived from a predic-
tive model, often taken from the activations of the final layer of a neural network, or a
latent space embedding [4, 9].
