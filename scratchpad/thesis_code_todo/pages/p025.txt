1.1     Outline of the thesis and contributions
Chapter 2 introduces molecular representations, model families, molecular similarity
and activity cliffs, and the sources of noise in molecular data. It defines the seven noise
conditions used in Chapter 3 and the three groups of methods for noisy labels (noise
detection, noise mitigation and noise-robust training), and closes with artificial noise
injection, and evaluation metrics.
   Chapter 3 looks into what makes a QSAR model robust to label noise. The pri-
mary prediction target is the HOMO–LUMO gap in QM9, whose computed labels
carry negligible measurement noise. The same experiments were also run on three as-
say datasets: LogD, Caco-2 and hERG Ki . We compare seven different types of label
noise, referred to as noise conditions. We matched the quantity of noise to the training
set label spread for all but one of the noise conditions. We measure noise robustness
as the proportion of predictive performance retained as artificial label noise increases.
We found that model family was the main factor in noise robustness, and choice of
molecular representation matters more for predictive performance than noise robust-
ness. Strong predictive performance on clean labels does not imply robustness. The
models with the highest predictive performance, LightGBM, XGBoost and the plain
neural networks lost the most with increasing label noise, while random forests were
the most consistent model across datasets, representations, and noise conditions. We
replicated systematic noise by adding it in disproportionate amounts to select scaffold
families and by clipping labels at a made-up assay limit. Models struggled the most
when training on this systematic noise. Label noise raised mean predicted uncertainty
for the probabilistic models which estimate it; however, only the BNNs with a vari-
ance head, the heteroscedastic GP and the heteroscedastic VBLL networks correctly
attributed the rise in uncertainty to the data rather than to the model itself.
   Chapter 4 takes a turn to look at another topic: molecular similarity. The natural
next step to Chapter 3 is noise mitigation. However, a small sweep of twelve published
noise-handling methods, four that remove molecules and retrain and eight that change
the training, showed disappointing results. None of the methods that incorporated
the concept of molecular similarity showed consistently promising results. On a given
dataset, standard representations carry many features that do not vary, and some map
different molecules to the same vector. Similarity computed over these representations
inherits both of these problems, and methods that rely on similarity suffer as a result.
