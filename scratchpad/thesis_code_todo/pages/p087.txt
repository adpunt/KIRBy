   Any method that incorporates similarity measures, such as AC detection, or clean-
ing a training set by comparing each sample’s label with its nearest neighbors’ labels,
whether by removing the samples that disagree [102] or by smoothing their labels,
inherits this choice, as do the noise estimation and mitigation methods of this thesis.
Coupry and Pogány [133] learn a molecular similarity from graph embeddings, but
independently of any endpoint. Zheng and Tropsha [134] did choose the similarity for
the task. They predict each molecule’s activity as the average over its k most sim-
ilar molecules by Euclidean distance over descriptors, and choose the descriptors by
simulated annealing to maximize the leave-one-out q 2 . Itskowitz and Tropsha [135]
also optimize the number of neighbors and their weighting. Both select features for
a nearest-neighbor model of one endpoint, whereas this thesis selects features across
several representations.
   The purpose of this chapter is to establish the problem that Chapter 5 sets out
to solve. Section 4.3.1 asks, for a given dataset, how much the features of standard
representations vary and how many molecules map to identical feature vectors. Sec-
tion 4.3.2 asks what happens when these features are used to quantify similarity, and
Section 4.3.3 follows the consequences for methods built on these similarity measures,
including a small sweep of published noise-handling methods (Section 4.3.3).
