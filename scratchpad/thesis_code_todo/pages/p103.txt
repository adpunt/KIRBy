5.1.1    Combining representations
Not every molecular representation is equal. In fact, they hold very different types
of information. RDKit2D descriptors matched or outperformed features derived from
embedded GNNs and pretrained language models on many datasets across 62,820 mod-
els on a variety of molecular property prediction benchmarks [108]. Descriptor-based
models held 73% of top-three positions on 11 benchmarks [158]. In 1,310 ChEMBL
assays, feedforward networks on ECFP fingerprints outperformed graph convolutions
and recurrent networks trained on SMILES [159]. We know that the choice of repre-
sentation matters, as is discussed in Chapter 3, yet the combinations of representation
and model are hard to explore by hand [160]. Beyond just molecules, general protein
language models predict antibody expression better than antibody-specific ones, while
the antibody-specific models are better on binding [161], and no pathology foundation
model is best on every clinical task [162]. We turn our attention to combining features
from these different representations.
   Most research in this field relies on a single representation, and few combine features
from different representations beyond concatenation. One of the reasons just pooling
features from different representations together doesn’t work is dimensionality. If you
add a standard ECFP4 (d = 2, 048) with Mordred descriptors (d = 1, 613) and an
MHG-GNN embedding (d = 1, 024), you end up with 4,685 features, which is far too
many considering many datasets from experimental assays contain only a few hundred
labeled samples. With such ratios, more features mean weaker predictions [163, 164].
Therefore, we consider ways of combining representations.
   The most straightforward method of combining representations is concatenation.
This however degrades with non-homogeneous feature sets and obscures which repre-
sentation is responsible for the score [165]. Deng et al. [108] found that appending
RDKit2D descriptors to GROVER lowered RMSE on 9 of 24 datasets tested. Late
fusion trains one model per source and aggregates the predictions [166]. MOGONET
is of this kind, combining per-source predictions in label space [167]. While this is
effective, it loses interactions between the source representations, an issue which is
addressed by intermediate fusion, for example cross-attention between sources [168].
Multiple kernel learning does this with kernels rather than whole models [169, 170],

ing bioactivity-based fingerprints with structure-based ones like ECFP4 improved both
predictive performance and scaffold-hopping. Similarly, Turkina et al. [172] combined
