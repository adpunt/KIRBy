\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}
% ABSTRACT LIMIT: 300 words maximum. Count the words before every submission build.
% [2026-10-10] trimmed to 297 words (was 425); spelling fixed.

This thesis explores the impact of label noise in molecular modeling. Much of the data in this domain is experimental, and the labels inherit noise from measurement error and differences between laboratories. Most research on label noise assumes Gaussian noise and considers few molecular representations and models. We show that non-Gaussian noise matters, both for predictive modeling and for the techniques meant to detect and mitigate it.

First, we investigate the effects of seven types of label noise across 13 models and six representations. In quantitative structure--activity relationship (QSAR) modeling, the representation has little impact on robustness to label noise, while the choice of model does. Random forests were the most consistently robust model. Systematic noise, such as labels clipped at an assay limit, did the most damage. Estimated uncertainty rose with noise but, outside censoring, did not identify individual corrupted labels.

Next, we tried several techniques for learning with noisy labels. Most features of a standard representation barely vary, and many others may not relate to the prediction task. Yet all of them contribute to the molecular distances that noise-aware techniques rely on.

To solve this issue, we developed the adaptive hybrid representation, which searches, for each dataset and model, for the best settings of a feature selection method across several molecular representations. On every small-molecule dataset, though not on all reaction data, the hybrid predicted better than every single representation, and its distances tracked activity more closely.

Finally, we compare single representations and hybrids under noise-aware training and noise mitigation. The methods that worked depend on the prediction task, the data, the model, and the underlying noise. The hybrids beat their base representations in almost every condition. The noise in a molecular dataset should thus be considered before modeling, and not assumed to be Gaussian.

\chapter*{Statement on the Use of Generative AI}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Statement on the Use of Generative AI}

I declare that my use of generative AI in this thesis complies with the University of Oxford's policy on generative AI in research and summative assessment. It also complies with the guidance of the Mathematical, Physical and Life Sciences Division for postgraduate research students and of the Department of Statistics. 

I developed the research questions, methods, experimental design, and results analysis independently, except where a citation says otherwise. I used generative AI to help structure my writing and within the review process; however, I did not have it generate direct text. I used generative AI to discuss research ideas and guide me through literature reviews; however, the contributions, specifically the adaptive hybrid search and the design of its search space, and the cliff-aware gap, a score for finding noisy labels, are my own. 

In terms of the specific generative AI used throughout my DPhil, I have primarily used Claude (Anthropic) through the Claude web application and Claude Code, from February 2026 to October 2026. The models were Claude Opus 4.5, 4.6, 4.7, 4.8, 5 and 5.5, Claude Sonnet 5 and 5.5, and Claude Fable 5 and 5.1. Before that, I used ChatGPT (OpenAI), with GPT-5.1 and GPT-5.2, from November 2025 to January 2026.

I wrote the original Python and Rust data pipelines by hand. I then used AI to extend the methods. I also used it to write plotting scripts, which run on code from recorded experimental outputs. That code is publicly available and the results and plots are reproducible. Generative AI was not used to make any figures directly from a prompt, nor was it used to generate or change any result. I drew all schematic diagrams using Lucidchart.
