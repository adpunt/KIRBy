	- Break it down by noise type - Gaussian noise is standard, but paradoxically it's actually one of the hardest to solve. Look at outlier noise, it's an objectively simpler task to identify a data point that's sticking out and remove it. With Gaussian noise, every sample has some form of noise, and you don't know how large/small it is or what direction it's going in. Trying to identify the ones with lots of noise is difficult, especially because if you make that selection based of "neighboring" molecules, those neighbors will also carry noise. Then do you remove it? Even if you correcrtly identify it are you confident which direction it goes in? I've seen plenty of noise mitigation methods produce a "clean" dataset that when a model is fit on it produces less acurate results than the "noisy" dataset it came from. There's a paradox about being able to successfully identify or estimate noise, but not being able to remove it. Improving similarity won't solve this problem
		- Go into practical take aways - you suspect your data's noisy, do you know what kind of noise? Does your data contain assay experiments from hundreds of labs (potential for ground-shifted or some other systematic noise), do you now know where it's sourced? Does your data contain molecules from vastly different domains? Maybe your data comes from a single controlled experiment but that may have censoring. If you don't know where your data is coming from or how much noise it has, what do you do? 
Some kind of conclusion to wrap it all together - focus on realisitic handling of noise - understanding how to model it, looking into the nuance. noise isn't just gaussian. And if it was, that's actually the hardest problem to solve
